18  AI: The AI predicts, you prove

A claim about what code does is a prediction to be verified.

The routine for this whole session is the same three-way move, repeated on harder and harder expressions until it is automatic. You predict what an expression evaluates to, by hand, doing the substitution and reduction steps yourself. You ask the assistant what it evaluates to, and read its answer, including whatever reasoning it gives. Then you run it through the %%steps widget, which is the only one of the three that actually reduces the expression step by step and cannot be talked around. Three answers, one witness. When your prediction and the assistant’s answer agree, that is pleasant and proves nothing. When they disagree, or when either of you turns out to be wrong, that is the session working exactly as intended.

TipThe three-way move, every time
  1. Predict. Write down your answer and the substitution/reduction steps that got you there, before you type anything into the assistant.
  2. Ask. Give the assistant the bare expression and ask what it evaluates to and why. Read its reasoning, not just its final answer.
  3. Prove. Run the expression through %%steps. Its trace is the verdict. Compare it against both of your answers and note any disagreement.

Every round below carries the AI: Explainer badge, which is the rung of the ladder you were given in week one and the only one you have so far. You are putting something in front of the assistant that already exists and asking it to explain that thing; you are not asking it to write anything, and you are not asking it to choose between two versions. What makes this session demanding is not the permission, which is the smallest one there is, but how hard you are going to work it. level one, twenty-two times, until checking a claim against a machine is what your hands do without being told.

Short-circuit values, not just True/False

and and or do not return True or False; they return whichever operand decided the outcome. This is the single richest source of a plausible-but-wrong AI answer in this whole session, because “and/or return booleans” is a very natural, very common, very wrong simplification.

Break the AI

Now you write the expressions. Working alone or with a partner, construct two expressions of your own, each combining precedence, comparison, and short-circuit logic, specifically designed to be the kind of case a fluent guesser would get wrong: something where the obvious-looking simplification (like “and/or return booleans”) fails. Trade your two expressions with another pair. For each expression you receive, do the full three-way move: predict, ask, prove.

Exercise 18-21

SOLO

Before trading anything, write down in one sentence why each of your two expressions is designed to trip up a plausible-but-wrong guess. This is the harder half of the exercise: constructing a good trap requires understanding the rule well enough to know exactly where it is easy to misapply.

Exercise 18-22

AI: Explainer

Run the full three-way move on the two expressions you received from the other pair. Report back to them, in one sentence each, whether their trap actually caught the assistant, caught you, caught neither, or caught both.

Logbook

For your logbook this week, report your personal tally across the whole session: how many of the roughly twenty expressions the assistant got wrong, how many you got wrong, and whether the two of you tended to get the same ones wrong or different ones. That last question matters more than the raw count, it tells you whether your instincts and the assistant’s failure modes overlap, which is exactly the thing you need to know to check it well.
