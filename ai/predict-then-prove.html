13  The AI predicts, the widget proves

A claim about what code does is a prediction, and a prediction is something to be checked rather than believed. This session runs entirely on last week’s material, so bring your notes on precedence and on substitution and reduction. Nothing new is introduced here. What is new is the habit, and the habit is worth a long session of its own because everything later in the course depends on it being automatic rather than deliberate.

The routine is the same three-way move, repeated on harder and harder expressions until you stop having to think about it. First you predict what an expression evaluates to, by hand, doing the substitution and reduction steps yourself and writing them down before you type anything into the assistant. Then you ask the assistant what the expression evaluates to and why, and you read its reasoning rather than skipping to its final answer. Then you prove it, by running the expression through the %%steps widget, which is the only one of the three that actually reduces the expression step by step and which cannot be argued with or talked around. Three answers, one witness.

Order matters here, and the order is not arbitrary. Predicting first is what makes the exercise worth anything, because once you have read somebody else’s answer you can no longer honestly discover what you would have thought. Asking second gives you a claim to check rather than an answer to accept. Proving last settles it. If you find yourself asking before predicting because predicting is slow, that is precisely the moment the session is trying to catch.

When your prediction and the assistant’s answer agree, that is pleasant and it proves nothing, since two guesses agreeing is not evidence. When they disagree, or when either of you turns out to be wrong once the widget has spoken, that is the session working exactly as intended. Keep a tally as you go, because you will need it at the end.

Warm-up with arithmetic precedence

Straightforward operator precedence, to get the three-way move running smoothly before anything tricky shows up. These are cases where you should be right every time, and the point of them is not the answers but the rhythm. Do the full move on every one, including the ones that look too easy to bother with, because the habit you are building has to survive contact with problems that look easy.

Comparisons and boolean logic

Chained comparisons, and the precedence of not, and, and or. This is a place where fluent-sounding wrong answers are common, because the rules read as obvious right up until you meet a case that breaks the obvious reading. It is also a place where your own intuition is unreliable in a different way from the assistant’s, which makes the comparison between the two more interesting than it was in the warm-up.

What and and or actually return

The single richest source of a plausible but wrong answer in this whole session. In Python, and and or do not return True or False. They return whichever operand decided the outcome. The simplification that they return booleans is natural, common, repeated in a great deal of writing about programming, and wrong, which makes it exactly the kind of thing a machine trained on a great deal of writing will tell you with confidence.

Predict the actual value in every case here, not merely whether the result is truthy. That distinction is the whole point of the round.

Before predicting or proving anything, ask the assistant to state in one sentence what and and or return in Python, and write its answer down word for word. Only then run the four expressions above through the widget and check its general claim against all four actual results. Does its one-sentence rule survive contact with every case, or only with the ones where the truthy shorthand happens to give the same answer? This is a small version of something you will meet all term, which is that a general claim can be right about the common cases and wrong about the rule.

Break the assistant

Now you write the expressions. Working alone or with a partner, construct two expressions of your own, each combining precedence, comparison, and short-circuit logic, and each designed specifically to be the kind of case a fluent guesser gets wrong. A good trap is one where the obvious-looking simplification, of the sort you caught earlier in the session, produces a confident answer that the widget then refutes. Trade your two expressions with another pair, and run the full three-way move on the two you receive.

Exercise 13-21

SOLO

Before trading anything, write down in one sentence why each of your two expressions is designed to trip up a plausible but wrong guess. This is the harder half of the exercise. Constructing a good trap requires understanding the rule precisely enough to know exactly where it is easy to misapply, which is a sharper form of understanding than being able to apply it correctly yourself.

Exercise 13-22

AI: Comparer

Run the full three-way move on the two expressions you received from the other pair. Report back to them, in one sentence each, whether their trap actually caught the assistant, caught you, caught neither, or caught both.

For your logbook this week, report your personal tally across the whole session: how many of the roughly twenty expressions the assistant got wrong, how many you got wrong, and whether the two of you tended to get the same ones wrong or different ones. That last question matters more than the raw count, because it tells you whether your instincts and the assistant’s failure modes overlap. If they do, you are a poor checker of exactly the cases you most need to check, and knowing which cases those are is worth more than any number of correct answers.
